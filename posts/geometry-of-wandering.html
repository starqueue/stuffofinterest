Essay № 03 · On thinking

On the geometry of wandering thoughts

When I trace the path my mind took across an hour, it always looks more like a coastline than a corridor — and that, I think, is the whole point.

Andrew Stuart 28 April 2026 7 min read

There's an old idea, popular among people who have never had to do any actual thinking, that a good mind moves in straight lines. It begins with a question, marches through a sequence of considered points, and arrives — neatly, on time — at a conclusion. This is the geometry of staircases: deliberate, sequential, slightly out of breath at the top.

But when I sit down at the end of a long walk and try to recall what I actually thought about, the shape that emerges is nothing like a staircase. It's more like the edge of a continent. There are headlands and inlets, the occasional spit of land that points hopefully into the sea before pulling back. There are bays that you mistake for the end of the journey until you round them. There are places where the mind seems to have eroded an entire coastline of certainty in a single gust.

The honest record of an hour's thinking is rarely a paragraph. It is more often a map.

What I've come to suspect is that the wandering is the thinking. The cul-de-sacs, the doublings-back, the moment when you remember a conversation from 2014 and somehow end up reconsidering your career — these aren't detours from the real work. They are the work. The corridor model assumes we know where we're going. The coastline model assumes we are, instead, finding out what's there.

A note on walking

I do most of my best thinking on foot, and I'm convinced this isn't a coincidence. Walking provides exactly the right amount of cognitive load to occupy the part of the mind that interrupts: the part that asks, every few minutes, are we doing this right? When that part is busy navigating a kerb, the rest of the mind can drift in peace.

Sit at a desk and you will think the thoughts you intended to think. Walk for forty-five minutes and you will think the thoughts you didn't know you were going to need.

Against the corridor

The reason I'm writing this down — apart from the obvious, which is that I have a blog and it must be fed — is that I keep meeting people who feel guilty about how they think. They report their wandering as a flaw. They apologise for it the way you might apologise for a stutter. Sorry, I'm getting off track. But they aren't off track. They're just on a longer one.

If your mind keeps wandering, the question to ask is not how to stop it. The question is what your mind keeps wandering toward. There's information in the shape of the coastline. Map it.

The corridor, of course, has its uses. When the building is on fire, you want a hallway, not a peninsula. But most of the thinking we do isn't an emergency. Most of it is a long afternoon by the sea, and the only real failure is to insist on walking in a straight line when the land itself doesn't.

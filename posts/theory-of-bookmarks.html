A small theory of bookmarks

We don't save things to read them. We save them to feel, briefly, like the kind of person who would.

Andrew Stuart 19 April 2026 5 min read

My read-later folder has, at last count, eight hundred and forty-three articles in it. Some of them have been there since 2017. I will not read them. We both know I will not read them. And yet: I keep saving more.

This used to bother me. I thought of it as a quiet failure of discipline — a sort of intellectual hoarding. But I've come to suspect something gentler about the impulse. The bookmark, I think, is not an act of future reading. It's an act of present identification. When I save an essay on the history of typography or a long-form piece on freshwater eels, I am not really planning to read it. I am voting, in a small private way, for the kind of mind I'd like to have.

The shelf as costume

People used to do this with their bookshelves. The visible spines were partly for reading and partly for signalling: here is what I care about, here is who I take myself to be. The unread book on the shelf is one of literature's oldest jokes — but it isn't really a lie. It's an aspiration with a dust jacket.

A bookmark is an aspiration with a URL.

What's changed isn't the impulse. What's changed is that the shelf has become invisible. No one sees my Pocket queue. There's no one to perform for. Which makes the question more interesting: who am I performing for, when I save a 9,000-word essay on the metaphysics of the loom at 11:48 on a Tuesday night?

The answer, I think, is me. I am performing for myself.

Three kinds of saved thing

I've started to notice that not all bookmarks are the same. There are at least three species:

  • The reference. Things I genuinely will return to: a recipe, a syntax cheatsheet, a paper I'll cite. These earn their keep.
  • The ticket. Things I save with the intention of reading, and sometimes do. These are honest bookmarks. They have a half-life of about a week.
  • The talisman. Things I save because the saving itself completed the transaction. The article was about a kind of person I'd like to be, and saving it briefly turned me into that person. The reading would have been redundant.

The third kind is by far the most common, and the one I used to feel guilty about. I no longer do. The talisman bookmark isn't a failure of follow-through. It's a tiny ritual of self-direction — a stone left on a cairn pointing toward who I'm trying to become.

So: eight hundred and forty-three. I'm not going to read them. But I'm not going to delete them either. They're a kind of map of the person I keep meaning to be. And every now and then, against all odds, I do become him — for an afternoon, with a cup of tea and an article about eels.
